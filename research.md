## Work in Progress

Aireti, D. and Yamazaki, K. “Prenatal War Exposure and Birthweight: Evidence from War-Affected Regions in Sri Lanka.”

*This paper analyzes the effects of in utero war exposure on birthweight using original survey data collected in war-affected regions of Sri Lanka. While earlier studies find that prenatal exposure to violence negatively affects birth outcomes, relatively little attention has been paid to gender-specific in utero selection and its implications for the estimated effects among live births. Using within-sibling comparisons, we account for shared family-level characteristics and further address potential gender-specific selection into live birth by distinguishing comparisons by sibling sex composition. We find that exposure to violence during pregnancy reduces birthweight, with effects varying by the child’s gender and type of violence. In particular, prenatal exposure to property damage reduces the birthweight of girls by approximately 293 grams, whereas the estimated effects are smaller and statistically insignificant for boys and for exposure to human victimization or forced displacement. One possible explanation is a culling effect that prenatal exposure disproportionately prevents less healthy male fetuses from surviving to live birth, leaving the surviving boys with stronger health endowments at birth than the girls in our sample. Our findings also suggest that the negative effect of prenatal war exposure on birthweight is driven primarily by maternal undernutrition during pregnancy rather than maternal stress.*
